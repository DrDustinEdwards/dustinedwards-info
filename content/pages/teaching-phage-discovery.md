The Phage Discovery Program is Tarleton State University's two-semester undergraduate research program in the HHMI SEA-PHAGES collaboration. Students isolate bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) from soil, purify them, image them, extract their DNA, send the best for sequencing and annotate the genomes. This page describes the program and then works as a phage discovery guide, stage by stage, that goes past the SEA-PHAGES Phage Discovery Guide with what this lab has learned from years of student notebooks. It is for students in the program and for instructors running phage discovery elsewhere. Every phage the program has found is in the [phage table](/research/phages), and the research side of the program, including the genome announcements students co-author, is on the [science education](/research/science-education) page.

## The program

We are part of the SEA-PHAGES national collaboration in bacteriophage genomics, run by the Science Education Alliance of the Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI) together with the University of Pittsburgh. Member schools follow a shared workflow, published as the SEA-PHAGES Phage Discovery Guide, and report every phage to [PhagesDB](https://phagesdb.org/). More about the national program is at [seaphages.org](https://seaphages.org/). At Tarleton the program is two discovery-based research courses, both taught as Special Topics (BIOL 4090).

**First semester (fall): [Virus Isolation](/teaching/virus-isolation).** Students collect soil or water samples and bring them to the lab. They run direct and enriched isolation assays to find phages, purify a phage by serial dilution and plaque assays, and amplify it to a high titer. They then characterize it: the phage is negatively stained on a grid and imaged by transmission electron microscopy to find its morphology, and its DNA is extracted and checked by restriction digest. Each phage is archived and entered on PhagesDB, and if the DNA is unique and of high quality and quantity, it goes to the Pittsburgh Bacteriophage Institute for whole-genome sequencing.

**Second semester (spring): [Phage Bioinformatics](/teaching/phage-bioinformatics).** Students annotate the whole genome of the sequenced phages: they find the genes, choose their start sites and assign functions from the evidence, and the finished annotation is submitted to GenBank. A class can then write the genome up as a short genome announcement. After both courses, students can keep characterizing their phages as independent research.

The hosts used in this lab have been *Mycobacterium smegmatis* (2017 notebooks) and, from 2018 on, *Microbacterium foliorum*. One notebook used *Mycobacterium fortuitum*.

## The phage discovery guide

The stages below follow the SEA-PHAGES Phage Discovery Guide. At each one, this page notes what the official guide covers and adds what this lab has learned: variations, fixes and mistakes to avoid. It adds to that guide; it does not replace it. The step-by-step bench methods are on the [phage isolation protocol](/research/protocols/phage-isolation) and the [phage DNA extraction protocol](/research/protocols/phage-dna-extraction) pages, so they are not repeated here.

## Stage 1: Collecting samples

The Phage Discovery Guide covers how to collect soil. What this lab adds:

- **How samples are taken.** Soil is dug from 2 to 3 inches down (one team took it from 6 to 10 inches) into a 15 ml conical tube filled two-thirds full, or about 25 ml in a 50 ml tube. Samples are refrigerated at 4 C until they are processed, from the next day to 3 days later. Ambient temperatures at late-August and early-September collection have run 22 to 36 C.
- **Where phages turned up.** Samples have come from Erath County, Texas, and neighboring counties (Hood, Johnson, Parker, Hamilton, Somervell and Eastland), plus Collin County. Students chose wet river banks, compost and animal areas on purpose.
- **What yielded phage.** Productive samples were often animal-associated: goat and horse lot soil with feces (*M. smegmatis*, 2017), soil under hay feed in a barn (2019), soil with sheep feces in a barn (2020), soil under horse manure and under a rotting hay bale, and water from the bottom of a hay trough, plated by direct isolation, with plaques in 24 hours (2022). Anthill and ant-bed soil has given several phages, by both direct and enriched isolation. A riverbank, a home garden, a community garden (which gave several phages) and roadside soil have also been positive.
- **What did not.** Clean pond, lake and stream water gave nothing across several semesters, and so did AC runoff and parking-lot rain. One semester's unusual samples (crushed termites, a ground wasp nest, shopping-cart handle swabs, dumpster debris, grass clippings, river water) were all negative, although phage [Loca](/research/publications/10-1128-mra-00783-22/) did come from a shopping-cart handle swab in another year.
- **GPS for PhagesDB.** A phage cannot go to GenBank without the collection GPS, and PhagesDB wants decimal degrees. Phone compass apps often give degrees, minutes and seconds, which must be converted (decimal = degrees + minutes/60 + seconds/3600). Notebooks have recorded coordinates with commas inside a number, a missing minus sign on the longitude, "E" for west, degree marks in place of minutes, seconds over 60, or no coordinates at all for the one positive sample. Check the format at the collection site, before it goes to PhagesDB.
- **A dead end.** One group plotted PhagesDB discovery data (coordinates, depth, temperature) on a map with pins colored by cluster. It showed nothing useful, so mapping phages by cluster was dropped.

## Stage 2: Isolation and purification

Direct isolation, enrichment, spot tests, plaque purification and making a high-titer lysate are on the [phage isolation protocol](/research/protocols/phage-isolation) page.

### When no phage turns up

Many students find nothing. In this lab, after several negative direct and enriched isolations (often 4 to 7 samples, sometimes 8 to 10), a student or group adopts a phage from a classmate: a plaque picked from a positive direct-isolation plate, a positive plate, or a positive enrichment. The adopted phage gets its own sample number and a new name, is carried through purification as the student's own, and the notebook copies the source sample's collection data and data sheet as the phage's origin. About half the notebooks in one semester did this. The same practice covers a group that cannot get a phage through purification after repeated failed samples or dilutions: it adopts another group's plate and continues from picking a plaque.

Adoption is a schedule fallback, not a guarantee. One group adopted another student's isolate after about four weeks of negative samples; the adopted filtrate also failed, and their own new sample was positive a week later.

## Stage 3: Electron microscopy

The guide covers TEM grid preparation. In this lab, grids are prepared and stained on site, then taken or shipped to an outside EM facility for imaging.

- **Concentrating the phage.** 1 ml of high-titer lysate is spun 1 h at 4 C at top speed (13,000 rpm on the lab's microfuge; notebooks give about 12,000 to 15,000 rpm), and the pellet is resuspended in 100 µl phage buffer for 30 to 60 min at 4 C. Variants that worked: 40 min at 25 C then 20 min at 4 C, or 15 min of gentle inversion. An overnight resuspension was judged too long and redone. For low titers, four 1 ml tubes were pelleted at 14,000 x g for 1 h, pooled into one tube and spun again.
- **Staining.** 10 µl on a grid for 2 min, two 10 µl water rinses of 2 min, then 10 µl 1% uranyl acetate for 2 min. Time on the grid is set by titer: 2 min at 2.3 to 2.9 x 10^9, 5 min at about 10^8, and 5 to 7 min for titers such as 6.4 x 10^7 or 3.6 x 10^8. Skipping the second water rinse left salts that showed on the TEM images.
- **Safety.** Uranyl acetate work is done in the fume hood with gloves, with no burner, and the instructor sets up the work area.
- **Storage.** Each finished grid goes in a numbered slot of the lab's grid box (for example A2 or B5), the slot is recorded in the notebook, and the box is kept in a vacuum-sealed container or desiccator (unseal, place, reseal) until it goes to the EM facility. Images have been captured with Gatan Digital Micrograph.

An example result: a siphovirus with a 102.3 nm tail and a 42.8 nm capsid.

## Stage 4: DNA extraction

The extraction itself is on the [phage DNA extraction protocol](/research/protocols/phage-dna-extraction) page. Keep enough DNA from the same extraction for an uncut lane before starting the digests; one run ran out and could not be used.

## Stage 5: Restriction digests and gels

The guide lists enzymes only for *M. smegmatis* and *Arthrobacter globiformis* phages. For classroom exercises on why digests are used to screen phage DNA, see [Understanding Restriction Enzyme Digests](/research/publications/10-25334-8cxe-6883/). These are the sets this lab has used:

| Host | Enzymes |
| --- | --- |
| *Mycobacterium smegmatis* (2017) | BamHI, ClaI, EcoRI, HaeIII, HindIII, SalI |
| *Microbacterium foliorum* | HaeIII, NspI, SacII, SalI; MseI added in 2023; HindIII in some notebooks |
| *Mycobacterium fortuitum* | HaeIII, MseI, NspI, SacII, SalI |

### Buffers and inactivation

- HaeIII, NspI and SacII in CutSmart (rCutSmart) buffer; SalI in NEBuffer 3.1; HindIII in 2.1. The 2017 digests used NEB buffers 3.1, CutSmart, EcoRI buffer and 2.1, for 60 min. Match the buffer by the color code on the tubes.
- Digest at 37 C for 1 h, or 15 min with time-saver enzymes. One set of notes ran 1.5 h.
- Heat-inactivate per NEB before storing: HaeIII 80 C 20 min; NspI, SacII and SalI 65 C 20 min.
- HindIII failed to cut one phage and was dropped. Some runs did not cut at all, so be ready to research other enzymes.
- Digest lambda DNA alongside as a positive control for the enzymes.

### Reaction setups used

- 50 µl: 38 µl diH2O, 5 µl 10X buffer, 1 µl enzyme, 6 µl DNA.
- 25 µl: 0.25 to 1 µg DNA, 2.5 µl buffer, 0.5 µl enzyme, water to volume. At 1,879 µg/ml, 0.5 µg is 0.27 µl, so a master mix was made for 7 tubes (3.5 µg = 1.86 µl DNA + 152 µl water, 22 µl per tube).
- 20 µl: 3 µl DNA, 2 µl buffer, 1 µl enzyme, 14 µl water; or 1.2 µl DNA, 2.5 µl buffer, 1 µl enzyme, 15.3 µl water.
- 10 µl: 2.7 µl DNA, 1 µl buffer, 1 µl enzyme, 5.3 µl water.

### Blank or faint lanes

The most common digest failure in the notebooks is too little DNA.

- Check the arithmetic for 0.5 µg: 0.5 µg at 110 ng/µl is 4.5 µl, and at 75 ng/µl about 7 µl. A digest set up with 0.2 µl of DNA showed only the ladder; redone with 4 µl it gave faint bands.
- Pipetting 0.2 to 0.5 µl of a stock near 1 µg/µl gave blank gels. Raising the DNA to about 2.5 µg (1.4 to 2.7 µl) gave sharp bands. Other fixes that worked: about 2 µl of a 2,467 ng/µl prep, 8 µl of DNA per 25 µl reaction, 2 then 4 µl per reaction at about 200 ng/µl, and 8 µl of a 62.4 ng/µl dilution where 1 µl gave nothing.
- Use the enzyme's own reaction buffer. 10X TBE pipetted in place of the reaction buffer gave no cutting on three gels.
- Digests stored too long before the gel showed no bands until they were digested fresh.
- A smear at the far end of a lane was read as methylation, and that DNA was accepted for sequencing.

### The gel

- **0.8% agarose:** 0.24 g in 30 ml 1X TBE, 0.32 g in 40 ml, or 0.4 g in 50 ml. Microwave about 1 min in 30 s bursts with a swirl, cool 10 to 15 min to 50 to 60 C, add 1.5 µl EtBr (10 mg/ml) per 30 ml for 0.5 µg/ml, and let it set 20 to 30 min with a 10-well comb. Weighing 3.2 g instead of 0.32 g (ten times too much) was a recurring slip, and DNA in those gels stayed near the wells.
- **Ladder:** lambda HindIII DIGEST, not HindIII enzyme; one gel lost its ladder to that mix-up. Mixes used: 1 µl ladder + 24 µl water + 5 µl 6X dye, or 1 µl + 7 µl water + 2 µl dye. A 1 kb ladder was also used (5 µl + 2 µl dye).
- **Loading:** add 5 µl 6X dye to each digest, heat at 65 C for 5 min, put on ice, then spin 15 s at 10,000 rpm. Loads of 20 µl are common, but they overflowed some wells and smeared the ladder, so 10 µl (with 1 µl ladder + 1 µl dye) or 5 µl per lane is an option. A phone flashlight under the gel box shows the wells.
- **Running:** 100 V for 1 to 1.5 h, until the dye reaches the 5 to 6 mark on the lab's gel box. One semester ran at 70 V. Running buffer is poured back for reuse. Image under UV.
- **Rescuing faint gels:** re-stain 5 min in running buffer with EtBr, then 10 min in buffer; or post-stain in 40 to 150 ml buffer or water with 4 to 6 µl EtBr on a rocker for 10 to 15 min, then 10 to 15 min (twice) in DI water. Post-staining did not rescue every gel. An over-stained gel can be destained in 1X TBE for 15 to 25 min, changing it every 5 min, or overnight in buffer.

### Uncut DNA does not give genome size

Two notebooks estimated genome size from uncut DNA against a lambda-HindIII ladder on a 0.8% gel (about 19,000 and 22,000 bp). Uncut phage genomes near or above the 23 kb top band cannot be sized that way, so do not record those figures as genome length. The genome length comes from sequencing.

## Stage 6: Archiving

The guide's archive uses 4 ml of high-titer lysate with 280 µl DMSO (about 7% DMSO). At Tarleton:

- The lysate and DMSO are mixed by inversion in a 15 ml conical inside the fume hood, where the DMSO is kept, then 1 ml goes into each of three tubes with a 2 ml serological pipette. Check the phage's entry on PhagesDB first.
- Two barcoded tubes are held at 4 C to ship to Pittsburgh, and a third, unbarcoded tube stays in the Tarleton freezer (frozen immediately, or at -20 C short term). Finished tubes go to the instructor for storage.
- Aim for a high-titer lysate, which the current [SEA-PHAGES Phage Discovery Guide](https://discoveryguide.seaphages.org/) defines as at least 5 x 10^9 pfu/ml (Protocols 6.4 and 6.5). One phage was archived at about 10^7 pfu/ml, far below it.
- Check the bottle: DMF was once used by mistake for DMSO.

Older practice, before the current guide: through 2021 the tubes were filled with sterile glass or cryo beads to about 1.5 cm below the rim (or three-quarters full), with the lysate and DMSO just covering them. Volumes were 1.4 ml + 100 µl DMSO split into three 400 µl tubes (2017), 2.8 ml + 200 µl (2019 to 2021) and 4 ml + 280 µl (2021 on). Beads were hard to load and were moved with a cut pipette tip and a conical-tube lid. By 2022 the lab used no beads.

## Stage 7: Sequencing

DNA that is unique by its digest pattern, and of high enough quality and quantity, is sent to the Pittsburgh Bacteriophage Institute for whole-genome sequencing. The finished sequence and its cluster assignment appear on the phage's PhagesDB page.

## Stage 8: Genome annotation

In the spring course, the class annotates the sequenced genomes. Annotation now happens in PHEONA, the HHMI web-based annotation tool, following the current SEA-PHAGES [Phage Genomics Guide](https://genomicsguide.seaphages.org/), whose June 2026 update removed DNA Master from the workflow. The steps, from downloading the genome to the GenBank submission, are on the [Phage Bioinformatics](/teaching/phage-bioinformatics) course page.

Older practice, before PHEONA: the class notebooks record an earlier workflow. The class started from the Starterator report, annotated each gene in PECAAN with evidence from HHpred, GeneMarkS, Phamerator, PhagesDB and NCBI BLAST and transmembrane prediction, checked for tRNAs with tRNAscan-SE, and exported the annotation from PECAAN into a DNA Master (.dnam5) file. An instructor pass, sometimes months later, re-added or removed evidence and settled start sites. The notes below come from genomes annotated that way, and the problems they describe still come up.

### Annotation problems that come up

- **Tail assembly chaperone frameshift.** Many phages make the tail assembly chaperone pair by a programmed -1 translational frameshift. Compare the protein pair of an annotated relative in the same phams (for example, two genes sharing an N-terminus), confirm the -1 frameshift, then find the slip site with the Six Frame Translation tool. Examples from class genomes: nt 22523 (genes 35/36), nt 16427 (genes 23/24), and nt 16856 in [Tripl3t](https://phagesdb.org/phages/Tripl3t/) (cluster A1). SEA-PHAGES notes say cluster EA2 has no known frameshift, which was the case for [Finny](https://phagesdb.org/phages/Finny/).
- **Genes the auto-annotation missed or got wrong.** A primase split in two was completed by adding the second gene by hand (39841-39326 in one genome, 40715-40201 in another), using relatives in the same pham and Six Frame Translation. An extra reverse gene was added (40064-39984). An orpham in the wrong reading direction, and a gene absent from the PhagesDB page, were deleted. Genes that only Phamerator showed, with no support in PECAAN, were not added.
- **tRNAs.** tRNAscan-SE predicted one 74 bp tRNA (42577-42504, anticodon CGT per PECAAN) in one genome and none in another.
- **Second looks.** HHpred structure hits to Cas4 on genes annotated as RecB-like exonuclease are worth a second look.

The annotated genomes are submitted to GenBank, and a class can then publish a short genome announcement. The site has a classroom guide to [writing Microbiology Resource Announcements](/research/publications/10-25334-b5bs-f125/). The announcements this program's students have co-authored are listed on the [science education](/research/science-education) page.

## Beyond the course

After both courses, students can keep characterizing their phages as independent research. Methods that have worked here:

- **Comparing many genomes.** To build a multi-genome FASTA, open the phage list on PhagesDB, sort by genome length to pull the set you want, download each phage's FASTA and join them in a plain-text editor. If the combined file will not load in MEGA X, rebuild it. Where a PhagesDB FASTA is not available, take the sequence from the GenBank accession linked on the phage page. Phages not yet annotated and submitted to GenBank cannot be included (82 of 83 made it in one project).
- **Whole-genome trees.** A neighbor-joining tree of 82 small-genome (about 15 kb) Arthrobacter phages, from clusters AN, AX and others, was built in MEGA X 10.0.5, then condensed by collapsing near-identical branches under one representative name.
- **Single-gene trees.** BLASTp the gene product on NCBI (one project used a LysM-like endolysin), export the hits as FASTA and build a p-distance tree with bootstrap values in MEGA X. A nucleotide version used BLASTn hits and the Tamura-Nei model. Candidate homologs across phages, bacteria and plants were first found with PhagesDB and Phamerator, then pulled from GenBank.
- **Tree methods can disagree.** For a putative Cas4 gene in [Arlo](https://phagesdb.org/phages/Arlo/), MEGA X maximum-likelihood trees gave no support while a neighbor-joining tree did. The top HHpred hits shifted over weeks (Cas4 to Csa1), and PHYRE2 result links expire, so save results when you get them. IQ-TREE, TreeFam, SplitsTree and GToTree were dropped because they needed coding.
- **Structure comparison.** Model the gene product in Phyre2 alongside a reference domain structure (one project used BMRB entry 19799, a LysM peptidoglycan-binding domain) and a bacterial homolog found by BLASTp, identify the domain with InterPro, and rotate the models to show the shared domain for a figure.
- **Genome maps.** Phamerator can align a small phage against a much larger one that shares a gene (here, to show both carry the endolysin); crop the larger genome in an illustration program for the figure.

## Working in the lab

- **Bench prep every session.** Spray CiDecon (diluted 1:128), wait 30 s and wipe with Kimwipes; spray 70% ethanol, wait 30 s and wipe dry; wash hands and change gloves. Work within about 8 inches of a lit Bunsen, propane or butane-canister burner, and flame the neck of the top agar bottle after opening and before capping. Skip the burner for DMSO (archiving) and uranyl acetate work, which are done in the fume hood.
- **Between lab days.** Plates are sealed with parafilm and refrigerated in an isolation box, and lysates go in the fridge's isolation bin at about 4 to 5 C. DNA extraction tubes have been left on ice or at -20 C over a weekend mid-protocol. Host aliquots of 250 µl in culture tubes are supplied ready-made.
- **Weekends.** The science building is closed at weekends, so plates set up on a Friday may come out of the incubator after only about 1 h. Returned to the incubator on Monday, they gave readable results the next day.
- **The notebook.** From 2019 the notebooks open with a sample table (collector, date, sample type, general location, description, GPS, depth, ambient temperature), then one entry per step with a Title, Date Started, Redo yes or no, Sample, Purpose, Notes (what was done, when, and anything different), Results with dates and photos, Conclusions and Next Steps. Record the EM grid slot. Research-project notebooks add Who and Materials (software and versions) to each entry, record dropped analyses as removed and archive them to the shared drive rather than delete them, and attach FASTA inputs.
